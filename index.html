Why does a protein fold?

Your DNA gives the order of amino acids (the protein's primary structure).

Every amino acid has a side chain. Polar side chains are attracted to water. Nonpolar side chains are oily and get pushed together, like oil droplets merging in salad dressing.

In the watery cell, the oily side chains tuck into the middle (the hydrophobic core) and the polar ones face the water. That makes the protein's 3D shape (tertiary structure), and the shape decides what the protein can do.

Model limits: this lab shows the biggest driver, the hydrophobic effect. Real proteins also use hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and helper proteins called chaperones. Real proteins have 100s of amino acids.

One tiny change, a different fold

Sickle cell disease starts with one DNA change. In β-globin (part of hemoglobin) it swaps position 6 from glutamic acid (polar) to valine (nonpolar). Below are the first 16 amino acids of normal β-globin (left) and a mutant copy (right).

Honesty check: this simple model can't predict how real hemoglobin sticks together, and in it the position-6 swap doesn't always stick out. What it does show: swapping one amino acid can change which beads end up inside or exposed, and where you swap matters. Folding has some randomness, so run each test a few times.

🏆 Denaturing challenges

Why does a protein denature?

A protein's shape is held together by weak bonds: the oily core, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges between + and − charged amino acids.

Heat makes molecules move faster until they shake those weak bonds apart. pH changes how many H⁺ ions are around: acids add H⁺ (− charges disappear), bases remove H⁺ (+ charges disappear). Then the salt bridges vanish and like charges repel.

Cold slows molecules down but doesn't unfold the protein.

Model limits: one small protein with simple rules. Real denaturation also depends on the protein: some survive boiling, and some refold when conditions return to normal.
